Cane Hollow

Supernatural horror · Family drama · 95–105 min · English

A thirteen-year-old girl who records everything is pulled with her fractured blended family into a Louisiana town that should not exist — and a house that survives by erasing strangers from memory.

Feature filmMemory · Family · Erasure

Logline

Tea Hills is thirteen and records everything. During a road trip to Austin with her fractured blended family, strange glitches lead them to Cane Hollow, an isolated Louisiana town that should not exist. At the Caron House, six places are waiting — and the town has survived for generations by sacrificing six travelers every ten years.

The story

Tea films because footage is her protection against adults denying what they said, rewriting what happened or simply moving on. When the family enters Cane Hollow, that habit becomes a survival tool. The Caron House does not merely kill its victims: it attacks the evidence that they ever existed.

Letters lose names. Seconds disappear. Faces blur. Objects move. Memories detach from the people who should own them. As the family tries to understand the rules of the house, Tea's camera becomes the last unstable archive of a reality being deliberately erased.

The house

The Caron House is the film's central antagonist. Its supernatural power is not limited to physical violence. It alters supports, spaces and memories, severing the links that allow the living to prove that the dead were ever there.

The six places

The house prepares six places for six travelers. The ritual has existed since 1875. Its most terrifying consequence is not simply death, but disappearance: a name can be offered, consumed and eventually removed from the memories that once carried it.

Tea's camera

Tea's recording habit is not a gimmick. It is the emotional counterforce to the house. She believes evidence can protect the truth; Cane Hollow is a place designed to prove that evidence can disappear too.

The family

The supernatural threat exposes an already fractured blended family: Rick, Annie, Mike, Kelly, Sierra and Tea each carries a different relationship to conflict, memory and responsibility. The house turns those fractures into weapons.

Authorial angle

Cane Hollow uses supernatural horror to explore a very human fear: what happens when your version of events can no longer be proven — and when the people who should remember you begin to forget.
